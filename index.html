My Story

An unusual path was the point.

My Duke journey, just like my academic pathway, is an unusual one. As a die-hard Blue Devil now, you might be shocked to learn I barely even considered Duke; I applied a week before the deadline and refused my counselor’s edits on my essay—not because I didn’t care, there was just something different about Duke. That little gut signal proved true when I got off the waitlist and visited campus for the first time. I knew this was where I was meant to be.

It took me a full year to find my footing. I was desperately homesick, academically lost, and felt like I was just going through the motions. Second semester of my first year, I took Designing Your Duke Journey, an Innovation & Entrepreneurship half-credit course. While I’ve seriously deviated from the path I had drawn for myself then, it was the first time I felt like I could find my niche at Duke.

After coding for the first time in my life, I completely changed my schedule heading into sophomore year. My background had always been visual: I grew up making art, then became increasingly interested in the narratives behind visual works through art history and English. Coding unexpectedly felt similar. I was still designing something—starting with a base and building until the pieces told a story or produced something useful.

The Computational Media IDM gave me a way to keep both instincts. Computer Science taught me how to build and interrogate systems; Visual Media Studies kept me from approaching those systems as purely technical objects; Economics gave me a framework for understanding their business implications. Together, they pulled me toward startups, commercialization, and eventually venture investing.

I don’t know the exact title I want decades from now, and I’m increasingly comfortable with that. I know I want difficult problems, constant learning, and work where technology and markets collide. Right now, that points toward investing in hard technology. Longer term, I’m interested in a JD/MBA and questions around AI ethics, persuasion, false advertising, and how information systems shape what people believe.

If Duke has taught me anything, it is that changing the plan is not evidence that the plan failed.
02

Coursework

From creating the pitch to questioning it.

My initial direction within I&E was marketing. It felt like an obvious extension of a visual background, and I had been told repeatedly that the skills I already had would translate well. I took Social Media Marketing and a global markets course while studying abroad.

Eventually, I realized I had confused being good at something with wanting to do it. Creating marketing could feel pedantic—the same exercise repackaged in a different font, sometimes literally. But the concepts behind it became much more useful once I stopped imagining myself as a marketer. Positioning, narrative, and consumer psychology became tools for evaluating companies rather than simply promoting them.

That shift became concrete at Silk. I entered an early-stage consumer technology company with a marketing background and moved toward market research, product strategy, and financial modeling. I became much more interested in whether the assumptions behind a story were true than in making the story itself prettier.

My I&E Keystone with Professor Shep Moyle pushed that further. The class operated closer to an MBA environment than a traditional undergraduate lecture: cold calls, presentations, workshops, and direct feedback from entrepreneurs and investors. When we pitched our faux bike companies to an experienced venture investor, the exercise made clear that a strong narrative matters—but only if the underlying argument survives questions.

I used those lessons almost immediately when pitching Silk at the Fuqua I&E Startup Showcase. Later, working in venture capital, I found myself on the other side of the same equation. Having helped build and pitch an early-stage company made me more skeptical of polished narratives because I understood how easily an insider can emphasize favorable information. Ironically, my marketing coursework became most valuable after I decided I had no desire to work in marketing.

EARLY-STAGE CONSUMER TECHNOLOGY

Silk AI Shopping

Learning to build—and let go.

When I joined Silk, it was an extremely early-stage startup built around a deceptively simple problem: online shopping had become fragmented. I initially joined with a market research focus, but at a company that early, job descriptions quickly lose their boundaries. I analyzed competitors, built market and financial models, worked alongside our founder on positioning and product strategy, and eventually created our pitch deck for a Harvard Business School pitch competition.

We entered with a strong conviction that extracting discounts from promotional emails would be central to Silk’s value. Users did appreciate having their inbox summarized, but our research increasingly pointed somewhere else: the little frictions surrounding a purchase—finding an order number, tracking a delayed package, noticing a price change, remembering a return deadline—were often more valuable to simplify than discovery itself.

That created a harder problem. Email may be clunky, but consumers have used it since the beginning of online shopping. Silk could smooth many of its frustrations, but asking someone to adopt an entirely new platform created friction of its own. The experience taught me that identifying a real problem is different from creating a behavior compelling enough for someone to change how they already solve it.

Pitching Silk also helped me realize I was more interested in sitting on the other side of the table—asking why certain companies worked, how trends created opportunities, and which assumptions within a pitch actually deserved to be believed.

STRATEGY & CONSULTING

Duke Consulting Club

Learning to structure ambiguity.

I originally joined Duke Consulting Club for a fairly practical reason: I wanted a job. Consulting seemed like a place where I could use quantitative analysis without abandoning the creative side of how I think. What I did not anticipate was how much it would change my ability to deal with ambiguity.

TheoVision — When the Best Model Isn't the Best Market

For TheoVision, our team developed a go-to-market strategy for an adjustable vision technology. We initially concentrated on NGOs and global vision-access organizations. I owned the grant-funding portion of the analysis, sourcing potential production grants and modeling how subsidy levels affected the economics. On paper, it worked. Operationally, reaching grant sponsors and NGO programs proved substantially harder for a for-profit company attempting to subsidize nonprofit distribution.

We ultimately shifted greater attention toward elderly care: less theoretically expansive, but with an identifiable customer, clear need, and a path to commercialization that did not depend on navigating the same grant ecosystem. The project taught me that a model can work and the recommendation can still be wrong.

United Way Greater Nashville — Building Within the Constraint

United Way presented almost the opposite challenge. The organization already knew the problem it wanted to solve; our job was determining how it should work. We interviewed existing incubators, researched the local nonprofit ecosystem, and translated those findings into a program built around a small cohort of growth-ready nonprofits, combining capital with executive coaching, peer learning, network access, and milestone-based development.

The project was less about discovering a dramatic pivot and more about learning to innovate within constraints: imposing enough structure around an idea to make it implementable without stripping away the flexibility that made it useful.
